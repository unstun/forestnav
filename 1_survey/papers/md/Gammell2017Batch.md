# Introduction {#sec:intro}

A fundamental task of any robotic system operating autonomously in dynamic and/or unknown environments is the ability to navigate between positions. The difficulty of this path planning problem depends on the number of possible robot positions (i.e., *states*) that could belong to a solution (i.e., the size of the *search space*). This search space is often continuous since robots can be infinitesimally repositioned and also often unbounded (e.g., planning outdoors) and/or high dimensional (e.g., planning for manipulation).

Most global path planning algorithms reduce this search space by considering a countable subset of the possible states (i.e., a *discrete approximation*). This simplifies the problem but limits formal algorithm performance to the chosen discretization. Popular discretizations in robotics include *a priori* graph- and anytime sampling-based approximations.

*A priori* graph-based approximations can be searched efficiently with informed algorithms, such as A\* [@hart_tssc68]. These informed graph-based searches not only find the optimal solution to a representation (i.e., they are *resolution optimal*) but do so efficiently. For a chosen heuristic, A\* is the *optimally efficient* search of a given graph [@hart_tssc68].

This efficient search makes informed graph-based algorithms effective on many continuous path planning problems despite a dependence on the chosen approximation. Sparse approximations can be searched quickly but may only contain low quality *continuous* solutions (if they contain any). Dense approximations alternatively contain high-quality continuous solutions [@bertsekas_tac75] but may be prohibitively expensive to search. Choosing the correct resolution *a priori* to the search is difficult and is exacerbated by the exponential growth of graph size with state dimension [i.e., the *curse of dimensionality*; @bellman_ams54; @bellman_57].

Anytime sampling-based approximations are instead built and searched simultaneously by sampling-based algorithms such as [PRMs]{acronym-label="PRM" acronym-form="plural+long"} [[PRM]{acronym-label="PRM" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @kavraki_tro96], [RRTs]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="plural+long"} [[RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @lavalle_ijrr01], and [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} [@karaman_ijrr11]. These sampling-based planners incrementally improve their approximation of the problem domain until a (suitable) solution is found (i.e., they have *anytime resolution*) and have probabilistic performance. The probability that they find a solution, if one exists, goes to unity with an infinite number of samples (i.e., they are *probabilistically complete*). The probability that algorithms such as [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and versions of [PRM]{acronym-label="PRM" acronym-form="singular+short"} ([e.g., [sPRM]{acronym-label="sPRM" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @kavraki_tra98] and [[PRMstar]{acronym-label="PRMstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}; @karaman_ijrr11]) asymptotically converge towards the optimum, if one exists, also goes to unity with an infinite number of samples [i.e., they are *almost-surely asymptotically optimal*; @karaman_ijrr11].

This anytime representation makes sampling-based planners effective on many continuous path planning problems despite a dependence on sampling. Incremental planners, such as [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}, generally search the problem domain in the order given by their random samples (Fig. [1](#fig:order){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:order"}). This simultaneously expands the search into the entire sampling domain (i.e., it is *space filling*) and almost-surely converges asymptotically to the optimal solution by asymptotically finding optimal paths to *every* state. This random order is inefficient and wastes computational effort on states that are not used to find a solution. This may be prohibitively expensive in many real problems, including unbounded or high-dimensional environments.

Previous work combining the complementary advantages of these two approaches has been incomplete. Sampling concepts have been added to informed graph-based search by either sacrificing anytime resolution or by decreasing efficiency by ordering the search on metrics other than solution cost. Heuristic concepts have been added to sampling-based planners by either also sacrificing anytime resolution or by decreasing efficiency by only applying heuristics to some aspects of the search.

This paper demonstrates how informed graph-based search and sampling-based planning can be directly unified and extended without compromise. It presents [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} as an example of an informed, anytime sampling-based planner that incrementally approximates continuous planning problems while searching in order of potential solution quality (Fig. [2](#fig:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:bitstar"}). This efficient approach avoids unnecessary computational costs while still almost-surely converging asymptotically to the optimum and finding better solutions faster than existing algorithms, especially in high state dimensions.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} approximates continuous search spaces with an *edge-implicit* [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @penrose_03]. This [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} is defined by a set of random samples and an appropriate connection condition. The accuracy of this approximation improves as the number of samples increases and almost-surely converges towards containing the optimal solution as the number of samples approaches infinity, similar to [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

This improving approximation is maintained and searched using heuristics. The initial approximation is searched in order of potential solution quality, as in A\*. When it is improved the search is updated efficiently by reusing previous information, as in incremental search techniques such as [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @koenig_ai04] and [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @aine_ai16]. The improving approximation is focused to the *informed set* of states that could provide a better solution, as in Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} [@gammell_iros14; @gammell_tro18].

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} only requires three user-defined options,

::: inparaenum
a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}-connection parameter,

the heuristic function, and

the number of samples per batch
:::

and can generalize existing algorithms (Fig. [3](#fig:taxonomy){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:taxonomy"}). With a single batch of samples and no heuristic, it is a search of a static approximation ordered by cost-to-come. This is exactly Dijkstra's algorithm [@dijkstra_59] on a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} or a version of [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @janson_ijrr15]. With a single batch of samples and a heuristic, it is a search of a static approximation ordered by estimated solution quality. This is exactly a lazy version of A\* [e.g., @cohen_rss14] on a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}. With multiple batches and a heuristic, it is a truncated incremental search of a changing approximation ordered by estimated solution quality. This is equivalent to a lazy version of [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} on a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} where replanning is always truncated after one propagation. With multiple batches of one sample and no heuristic, it is a construction of a tree through incremental sampling. This is equivalent to a '`steer`'-free version of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} where unsuccessful samples are maintained.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} can also be extended to create new planners. [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is presented as an extension of heuristically ordered search concepts to the incremental sampling of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} algorithms. A version of both [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} are publicly available in the [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @ompl].

The benefits of performing an ordered anytime search of the problem domain are demonstrated on both abstract problems and experiments for the [CMU]{acronym-label="CMU" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} Personal Robotic Lab's [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+short"}, a 14-[DOF]{acronym-label="DOF" acronym-form="singular+short"} mobile manipulation platform [@herb]. The results show that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds better solutions faster than existing almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners and also [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"}, especially in high dimensions. The only tested planner that found (worse) solutions faster was [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect [@kuffner_icra00], a bidirectional version of [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"} that is *not* almost-surely asymptotically optimal.

## Relationship to Previous Publications {#sec:intro:prev}

This paper is distinct from our prior work analyzing the necessary conditions for anytime sampling-based planners to improve an existing solution ["Informed sampling for asymptotically optimal path planning"; @gammell_tro18]. This other publication investigates focusing the search for improvements in anytime sampling-based planners. It presents both analytical concepts that are applicable to any cost function (e.g., informed sets) and algorithmic approaches specific to problems seeking to minimize path length (e.g., direct informed sampling of the $L^2$ informed set).

In comparison, this paper presents algorithmic approaches to order the search for both an initial solution and subsequent improvements. These techniques are applicable to any cost function, similarly to A\*, and make use of the appropriate analytical results from @gammell_tro18. The performance of these techniques are illustrated on a number of problems seeking to minimize path length (Section [6](#sec:exp){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp"}) where we make use of the appropriate algorithmic results of @gammell_tro18.

# Prior Work Ordering Sampling-based Planners {#sec:lit}

A formal definition of the optimal path planning problem is presented to motivate a review of existing literature on the combination of informed graph-based and sampling-based concepts (Section [2.1](#sec:lit:opt){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:lit:opt"}). This prior work can be loosely classified as either incorporating sampling into informed A\*-style searches (Section [2.2](#sec:lit:astar){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:lit:astar"}) or adding heuristics to incremental [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}/[RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-style searches (Section [2.3](#sec:lit:rrt){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:lit:rrt"}).

## The Optimal Path Planning Problem {#sec:lit:opt}

The two most common path planning problems in robotics are those of *feasible* and *optimal* planning. Feasible path planning seeks a path that connects a start to a goal in a search space while avoiding obstacles and obeying the differential constraints of the robot. Optimal path planning seeks the feasible path that minimizes a chosen cost function (e.g., path length). By definition, solving an optimal planning problem requires solving the underlying feasible problem.

The optimal path planning problem is defined in Definition [1](#defn:back:opt){reference-type="ref" reference="defn:back:opt"} similarly to @karaman_ijrr11. This definition is expressed in the state space of a robot but can be posed in other representations, including configuration space [@lozano-perez_tc83]. The goal of these problems may be a single goal state (e.g., a pose for a mobile robot) or any state in a goal *region* (e.g., the set of joint angles that give a redundant manipulator a desired end-effector position).

::: {#defn:back:opt .defn}
**Definition 1** (The optimal path planning problem). *Let $X\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ be the state space of the planning problem, $X_{\rm obs}\subset X$ be the states in collision with obstacles, and $X_{\rm free}= \mathrm{cl}\left(X\setminus X_{\rm obs}\right)$ be the resulting set of permissible states, where $\mathrm{cl}\left(\cdot\right)$ represents the closure of a set. Let $\mathbf{x}_{\rm start}\in X_{\rm free}$ be the initial state and $X_{\rm goal}\subset X_{\rm free}$ be the set of desired goal states. Let $\sigma: \; \left[0,1\right] \to X_{\rm free}$ be a continuous map to a sequence of states through collision-free space of bounded variation that can be executed by the robot (i.e., a collision-free, feasible path) and $\Sigma$ be the set of all such nontrivial paths.*

*The optimal path planning problem is then formally defined as the search for a path, $\sigma^{*}\in\Sigma$, that minimizes a given cost function, $c: \; \Sigma\to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$, while connecting $\mathbf{x}_{\rm start}$ to $\mathbf{x}_{\rm goal}\in X_{\rm goal}$, $$\begin{align*}
        \sigma^{*}= \mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\sigma\in \Sigma} \left\lbrace c\left(\sigma\right)\;\;\middle|\;\;\sigma\left(0\right) = \mathbf{x}_{\rm start},\, \sigma\left(1\right) \in X_{\rm goal}\right\rbrace,
\end{align*}$$ where $\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ is the set of non-negative real numbers.*
:::

## A\*-based Approaches {#sec:lit:astar}

A\* is a popular planning algorithm because it is the optimally efficient search of a graph. Any other algorithm guaranteed to find the resolution-optimal solution will expand at least as many vertices when using the same heuristic [@hart_tssc68]. This efficiency is achieved by ordering the search by potential solution quality such that the optimal solution is found by only considering states that could have provided a better solution. Applying A\* to continuous path planning problems requires discretizing the search domain and significant work has incorporated sampling into this process, often to avoid the need to do so *a priori*.

@sallaberger_acta95 demonstrate the advantages of including stochasticity in the representation of a continuous state space by adding random perturbations to a regular discretization. They solve path planning problems for spacecraft and multilink robotic arms using dynamic programming and show that their technique finds better solutions than regular discretizations alone; however, the approximation is still defined *a priori* to the search.

[RAstar]{acronym-label="RAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[RAstar]{acronym-label="RAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @diankov_iros07] uses random sampling to apply A\* directly to continuous planning problems. Vertices are expanded a user-specified number of times by randomly sampling possible nearby descendants. If a sample is sufficiently different than the existing states in the tree and can be reached without collision it is added as a child of the expanded vertex. The resulting sampling-based search expands outwards from the start in order of potential solution quality but is not anytime. If a (suitable) solution is not found then the search must be restarted with a different number of samples per vertex.

[HRAstar]{acronym-label="HRAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[HRAstar]{acronym-label="HRAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @teniente_iros13] performs a similar search for systems with differential constraints by sampling control inputs instead of states. Vertices are expanded a user-specified number of times by generating random control inputs and propagating them forward in time with a motion model. These new states are used both to expand a tree and rewire it when they are sufficiently similar to existing states. The vertex to expand is selected with a hybrid cost policy that considers path cost, the number of nearby vertices, and the distance to obstacles. This biases sampling into unexplored regions of the problem domain but may waste computational effort on states that are unnecessary to find the eventual solution.

[SBAstar]{acronym-label="SBAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[SBAstar]{acronym-label="SBAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @persson_ijrr14] performs an ordered search by *iteratively* expanding vertices in a heuristically weighted version of [EST]{acronym-label="EST" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[EST]{acronym-label="EST" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @hsu_ijcga99]. At each iteration, a *single* random sample is generated near the vertex at the front of a priority queue. This queue is ordered on the potential solution quality of vertices and the likelihood of sampling a *unique* and *useful* sample in their neighbourhoods. These likelihoods are required to bias sampling into unexplored space since vertices are never removed from the queue. They are estimated from previous collision checks and estimates of the sample density around vertices. Again, this search order on metrics other than the optimization objective may waste computational effort on states that are unnecessary to find the eventual solution.

Unlike these methods to incorporate sampling into informed graph-based search, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} maintains anytime performance while ordering its search only on potential solution quality. This allows it to be run indefinitely until a suitable solution is found, return incrementally better solutions, and almost-surely asymptotically converge to the optimum. This also limits its search of each representation to the set of states that were believed to be capable of providing a better solution than the one eventually found.

## [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-based Approaches {#sec:lit:rrt}

[RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} solve continuous path planning problems by using incremental sampling to build a tree through obstacle-free space. This avoids *a priori* discretizations of the problem domain and allows them to be run indefinitely until a (suitable) solution is found. This also makes the search dependent on the sequence of samples (i.e., makes it random) and significant work has sought ways to use heuristics to order the search. Heuristics can also be used to focus the search, as in Anytime [RRTs]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="plural+abbrv"} [@ferguson_iros06] and Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} [@gammell_iros14; @gammell_tro18] but this does not change the order of the search.

[hRRT]{acronym-label="hRRT" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[hRRT]{acronym-label="hRRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @urmson_iros03] probabilistically includes heuristics in the [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"} expansion step. Randomly sampled states are probabilistically added in proportion to their heuristic value relative to the tree. This balances the Voronoi bias of the [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"} search with a preference towards expanding potentially high-quality paths. This improves performance, especially in problems with continuous cost functions [e.g., path length; @urmson_iros03], but maintains a nonzero probability of wasting computational effort on states that are unnecessary for the eventual solution.

@karaman_icra11 and @akgun_iros11 both use heuristics to accelerate the convergence of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. @karaman_icra11 remove vertices whose *current* cost-to-come plus a heuristic estimate of cost-to-go is higher than the current solution. @akgun_iros11 reject samples that are heuristically estimated to be unable to provide a better solution. These approaches both avoid unnecessary computational effort but do not alter the initial search as heuristics are not applied until a solution is found.

@kiesel_socs12 use a two-stage process to order sampling for [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} in their *f-biasing* technique. A heuristic is first calculated by solving a coarse discretization of the planning problem with Dijkstra's algorithm. This heuristic is then used to bias [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} sampling to the regions of the problem domain where solutions were found. This increases the likelihood of finding solutions quickly but maintains a nonzero sampling probability over the entire problem domain for the whole search and allows search effort to be wasted on states unnecessary for the eventual solution.

[RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ [@arslan_icra13; @arslan_icra15] uses heuristics to find and update a tree in the graph built incrementally by [RRG]{acronym-label="RRG" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[RRG]{acronym-label="RRG" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @karaman_ijrr11]. It efficiently maintains the optimal connection to each vertex by using [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to propagate changes through the *entire* graph. This can also be implemented as policy iteration [@arslan_cdc16].

[RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ uses heuristics to limit this graph to regions of the problem domain that can improve an *existing* solution. This focuses the search for an improvement but does not alter the *order* in which the graph itself is constructed. As in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, this graph is built in the order given by the sampling sequence using [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"}-style incremental techniques. This may cause important, difficult-to-sample states to be discarded simply because they cannot currently be connected to the tree. It may also waste computational effort, especially during the search for an initial solution (Fig. [1](#fig:order){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:order"}).

[RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^X^ [@otte_wafr14; @otte_ijrr16] extends propagated rewiring to dynamic environments by limiting propagation to changes that improve the graph by more than a user-specified threshold. This *$\epsilon$-consistency* balances the local rewiring performed by [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and the cascaded rewiring performed by [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ to improve performance. As in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^, it does not order the construction of the graph itself.

[FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} [@janson_isrr13; @janson_ijrr15] uses a marching method to search a batch of samples in order of increasing cost-to-come, similar to Dijkstra's algorithm. This separation of approximation and search makes the search order independent of the sampling order but sacrifices anytime performance. Solutions are not returned until the search is finished and the search must be restarted if a (suitable) solution is not found, as with any other *a priori* discretization.

The [MPLB]{acronym-label="MPLB" acronym-form="singular+full"} algorithm [@salzman_icra15] extends [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} with a heuristic and quasi-anytime resolution. Quasi-anytime performance is achieved by *independently* searching increasingly large batches of samples and returning the improved solutions when each individual search finishes. It is stated that this can be done efficiently by reusing information but no specific methods are presented.

Unlike these methods to incorporate informed search concepts into single-query sampling-based algorithms, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} orders its entire search only on potential solution quality and still maintains anytime performance. This avoids wasting computational effort by only considering states that are believed to be capable of providing the best solution given the current representation. This also returns suboptimal solutions in an anytime manner while almost-surely converging asymptotically to the optimum.

# [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+full"} {#sec:bitstar}

Any discrete set of states distributed in the state space of a planning problem, $X_{\rm samples}\subset X$, can be viewed as a graph whose edges are given algorithmically by a transition function (an *edge-implicit* graph). When these states are sampled randomly, $X_{\rm samples}= \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\sim \mathcal{U}\left(X\right)\right\rbrace$, this graph is known as a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} and has studied probabilistic properties [@penrose_03].

The connections (edges) between states (vertices) in a [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} depend on their relative geometric position. Common [RGGs]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="plural+short"} have edges to a specific number of each vertex's nearest neighbours [a $k$-nearest graph; @eppstein_dcg97] or to all neighbours within a specific distance [an $r$-disc graph; @gilbert_siam61]. [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} theory provides probabilistic relationships between the number and distribution of vertices, the $k$ or $r$ defining the graph, and specific properties such as connectivity or relative cost through the graph [@penrose_03; @muthukrishnan_siam05].

Anytime sampling-based planners can be viewed as algorithms that construct a graph in the problem domain and search it. Some algorithms perform this construction and search simultaneously (e.g., [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}) and others separately (e.g., [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}) but, as in graph-based search, their performance always depends on both the accuracy of their approximation and the quality of their search. [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} uses [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} theory to limit graph complexity while maintaining probabilistic bounds on approximation accuracy but incompletely searches the graph in the order it is constructed (i.e., performs a *random* search). [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ and [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^X^ exploit the constructed graphs more thoroughly than [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} but do not alter the order of its construction (i.e., they depend on the same *random* search of the underlying problem domain). [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} performs a complete ordered search but uses [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} theory to define an *a priori* approximation of the problem domain (i.e., it is not anytime).

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} uses [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} theory to limit graph complexity while simultaneously building the graph in an anytime manner and searching it in order of potential solution quality. This is made possible by using *batches* of random samples to build an increasingly dense *edge-implicit* [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} in the informed set and using incremental search techniques to update the search (Fig. [4](#fig:example){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:example"}). The anytime approximation allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to run indefinitely until a (suitable) solution is found. The ordered search avoids unnecessary computational cost by only considering states when they represent the best possible solution to a given approximation. The incremental search allows it to update its changing representation efficiently by reusing previous information.

The complete [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} algorithm is presented in Algs. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}--[\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"} and Sections [3.1](#sec:bitstar:note){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:note"}--[3.7](#sec:bitstar:algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:prune"} with a discussion on some practical considerations presented in Section [3.8](#sec:bitstar:algo:pract){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:pract"}. For simplicity, the discussion is limited to a search from a single start state to a finite set of goal states with a constant batch size. This formulation can be directly extended to searches from a start or goal region, and/or with variable batch sizes. This version is publicly available in [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} builds an explicit spanning tree of the implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} defined by a batch of samples. The graph initially consists of only the start and goal (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startInit\]](#algo:bitstar:startInit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startInit"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endInit\]](#algo:bitstar:endInit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endInit"}; Section [3.2](#sec:bitstar:algo:init){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:init"}) but is incrementally grown with batches of new samples during the search (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startBatch\]](#algo:bitstar:startBatch){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startBatch"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endBatch\]](#algo:bitstar:endBatch){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endBatch"}; Section [3.3](#sec:bitstar:algo:batch){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:batch"}). The graph is searched in order of potential solution quality by selecting the best possible edge from a queue ordered by potential solution cost (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startNextEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:startNextEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startNextEdge"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endNextEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:endNextEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endNextEdge"}; Section [3.4](#sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge"}) and considering whether this edge improves the cost-to-come of its target vertex and could improve the current solution (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startProcess\]](#algo:bitstar:startProcess){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startProcess"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endProcess\]](#algo:bitstar:endProcess){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endProcess"}; Section [3.5](#sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge"}). The search continues until no edges in the implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} could provide a better solution, at which point the accuracy of the approximation is increased by adding a batch of new samples and the search is resumed. This process continues indefinitely until a suitable solution is found.

## Notation {#sec:bitstar:note}

The functions $\widehat{g}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ and $\widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ represent admissible estimates of the cost-to-come to a state, $\mathbf{x}\in X$, from the start and the cost-to-go from a state to the goal, respectively (i.e., they bound the true costs from below). The function, $\widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$, represents an admissible estimate of the cost of a path from $\mathbf{x}_{\rm start}$ to $X_{\rm goal}$ constrained to pass through $\mathbf{x}$, i.e., $\widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) := \widehat{g}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$. This estimate defines an informed set of states, $X_{\widehat{f}}:= \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\in X\;\;\middle|\;\;\widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) < c_{i}\right\rbrace$, that could provide a solution better than the current best solution cost, $c_{i}$ [@gammell_iros14; @gammell_tro18].

Let $\mathcal{T}:= \left( V, E\right)$ be an *explicit* tree with a set of vertices, $V\subset X_{\rm free}$, and edges, $E= \left\lbrace \left( \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{w}\right)\right\rbrace$ for some $\mathbf{v},\, \mathbf{w}\in V$. The function $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ then represents the cost-to-come to a state $\mathbf{x}\in X$ from the start vertex given the current tree, $\mathcal{T}$. A state not in the tree, or otherwise unreachable from the start, is assumed to have a cost-to-come of infinity. It is important to recognize that these two functions will always bound the unknown true optimal cost to a state, $g\left(\cdot\right)$, i.e., $\forall \mathbf{x}\in X,\,\widehat{g}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \leq g\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \leq g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$.

The functions $\widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)$ and $c\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)$ represent an admissible estimate of the cost of an edge and the true cost of an edge between states $\mathbf{x},\, \mathbf{y}\in X$, respectively. Edges that intersect an obstacle are assumed to have a cost of infinity, and therefore $\forall \mathbf{x},\, \mathbf{y}\in X,\,\, \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right) \leq  c\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right) \leq \infty$.

The notation $X\xleftarrow{\scriptscriptstyle +}\left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\right\rbrace$ and $X\xleftarrow{\scriptscriptstyle -}\left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\right\rbrace$ is used to compactly represent the set compounding operations $X\gets X\cup \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\right\rbrace$ and $X\gets X\setminus \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\right\rbrace$, respectively. As is customary, the minimum of an empty set is taken to be infinity.

## Initialization (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startInit\]](#algo:bitstar:startInit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startInit"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endInit\]](#algo:bitstar:endInit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endInit"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:init}

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} begins searching a planning problem with the start, $\mathbf{x}_{\rm start}$, in the spanning tree, $\mathcal{T}\coloneqq\left( V, E\right)$, and the goal states, $X_{\rm goal}$, in the set of unconnected states, $X_{\rm unconn}$ (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:initTree\]](#algo:bitstar:initTree){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initTree"}--[\[algo:bitstar:initSamples\]](#algo:bitstar:initSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initSamples"}). This defines an implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} whose vertices consist of all states (i.e., $V\cup X_{\rm unconn}$) and whose edges are defined by a distance function and an appropriate connection limit. When the goal is a continuous region of the problem domain it will need to be discretized (e.g., sampled) before adding to the set of unconnected states.

The explicit spanning tree of this edge-implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} is built using two queues, a vertex expansion queue, $\mathcal{Q}_{V}$, and an edge evaluation queue, $\mathcal{Q}_{E}$. These queues are sorted in order of potential solution quality through the current tree. Vertices in the vertex queue, $\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{Q}_{V}$, are ordered by the sum of their current cost-to-come and an estimate of their cost-to-go, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)$. Edges in the edge queue, $\left( \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}\right)\in\mathcal{Q}_{E}$, are sorted by the sum of the current-cost-to-come of their source vertex, an estimate of the edge cost, and an estimate of the cost-to-go of their target vertex, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$. Ties are broken in the vertex queue in favour of entries with the lowest cost-to-come through the current tree, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)$, and in the edge queue in favour of the lowest cost-to-come through the current tree and estimated edge cost, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\right)$, and then the cost-to-come through the current tree, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)$. These queues are initialized to contain all the vertices in the tree and an empty queue, respectively (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:initQueues\]](#algo:bitstar:initQueues){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initQueues"}).

To improve search efficiency, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} tracks the vertices in the goal region, $V_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize sol'n}}$, the vertices that have never been expanded, $V_{\rm unexpnd}$, the samples newly created during this batch, $X_{\rm new}$, and the current best solution, $c_{i}$. These are initialized to any vertices already in the solution (empty in all but the most trivial planning problems), the existing vertices, the existing samples, and the current best solution, respectively (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:initLabels\]](#algo:bitstar:initLabels){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initLabels"}--[\[algo:bitstar:initCmin\]](#algo:bitstar:initCmin){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initCmin"}).

Initialized, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} now searches the continuous planning problem by alternately building increasingly accurate implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} approximations (Section [3.3](#sec:bitstar:algo:batch){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:batch"}) and searching these representations for explicit solutions in order of potential solution quality (Sections [3.4](#sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge"} and [3.5](#sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge"}).

## Batch Addition (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startBatch\]](#algo:bitstar:startBatch){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startBatch"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endBatch\]](#algo:bitstar:endBatch){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endBatch"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:batch}

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} alternates between building an increasingly dense approximation of the continuous planning problem and searching this representation for a solution. The approximation is updated whenever it has been completely searched (i.e., both queues are empty; Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:emptyQueues\]](#algo:bitstar:emptyQueues){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:emptyQueues"}) by removing unnecessary states and adding a batch of new samples. This avoids the computational cost of representing regions of the problem domain that cannot provide a better solution while increasing the accuracy of approximating the regions that can (i.e., the informed set). This improving approximation allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to almost-surely converge asymptotically to the optimal solution.

The approximation is pruned to the informed set by removing any states or edges that cannot improve the *current* solution (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:prune\]](#algo:bitstar:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:prune"}; Section [3.7](#sec:bitstar:algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:prune"}). This reduces unnecessary complexity but may disconnect vertices in the informed set that cannot improve the solution solely because of their current connections. These vertices are recycled as additional 'new' samples in the batch so that they may be reconnected later if better connections are found.

The approximation is improved by adding $m$ new randomly generated samples from the informed set (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:sample\]](#algo:bitstar:sample){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:sample"}). This can be accomplished with direct informed sampling [@gammell_iros14; @gammell_tro18] or advanced rejection sampling [e.g., @kunz_icra16] as appropriate.

Each batch of states are labelled as $X_{\rm new}$ for the duration of that batch's search (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:resetNewSamples\]](#algo:bitstar:resetNewSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:resetNewSamples"}). This set is used to improve search efficiency and consists of both the newly generated samples and the recycled disconnected vertices. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} adds these new states to the set of unconnected states and initializes the vertex queue with all the vertices in the tree (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:addNewSamples\]](#algo:bitstar:addNewSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:addNewSamples"}--[\[algo:bitstar:resetVertexQueue\]](#algo:bitstar:resetVertexQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:resetVertexQueue"}) to restart the search (Sections [3.4](#sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge"} and [3.5](#sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge"}).

## Edge Selection (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startNextEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:startNextEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startNextEdge"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endNextEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:endNextEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endNextEdge"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge}

Graph-based search techniques often assume that finding and evaluating vertex connections is computationally inexpensive (e.g., given explicitly). This is not true in sampling-based planning as finding vertex connections (e.g., the edges in the edge-implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}) requires performing a nearest-neighbour search and evaluating them requires checking for collisions and solving [2BVPs]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="plural+short"}, such as differential constraints. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} avoids these computational costs until required by using a lazy search procedure that delays both finding and evaluating connections in the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}. Similar lazy techniques can be found in both advanced graph-based search and sampling-based planners [@bohlin_icra00; @sanchez_ijrr02; @helmert_jair06; @branicky_icra01; @cohen_rss14; @hauser_icra15; @salzman_tro16].

Connections are found by using a vertex queue, $\mathcal{Q}_{V}$, ordered by potential solution quality. This vertex queue delays processing a vertex (i.e., performing a nearest-neighbour search) until its outgoing connections *could* be part of the best solution to the current graph. Connections from all vertices are evaluated by using an edge queue, $\mathcal{Q}_{E}$, also ordered by potential solution quality. This edge queue delays evaluating an edge (i.e., performing collision checks and solving [2BVPs]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="plural+short"}) until it *could* be part of the best solution to the current graph.

A vertex in the vertex queue could be part of the best solution when it could provide an outgoing edge better than the best edge in the edge queue. When the heuristic is consistent (e.g., the $L^2$ norm) the queue value of a vertex, $\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{Q}_{V}$, is a lower-bounding estimate of the queue value of its outgoing edges, $$\begin{align*}
    \forall \mathbf{x}\in X,\; g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) \leq g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}\right).
\end{align*}$$

The best edge at any iteration can therefore be found by processing the vertex queue until it is worse than the edge queue (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:compareQueues\]](#algo:bitstar:compareQueues){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:compareQueues"}). This process of removing a vertex from the vertex queue and placing its outgoing edges in the edge queue is referred to as *expanding* a vertex (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue\]](#algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue"}; Section [3.6](#sec:bitstar:algo:expandVertex){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:expandVertex"}). Once all necessary vertices are expanded, the best edge in the queue, $\left( \mathbf{v}_{\rm min}, \mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)$, is removed (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:popEdgeQueue\]](#algo:bitstar:popEdgeQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:popEdgeQueue"}) and used for this iteration of the search (Section [3.5](#sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge"}). 

The functions $\mathtt{BestQueueValue}\left(\cdot\right)$ and $\mathtt{PopBestInQueue}\left(\cdot\right)$ return the value of the element at the front of a queue and pop the element off the front of a queue, respectively.

## Edge Processing (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:startProcess\]](#algo:bitstar:startProcess){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:startProcess"}--[\[algo:bitstar:endProcess\]](#algo:bitstar:endProcess){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:endProcess"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:addEdge}

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} also uses heuristics to avoid expensive calculations when evaluating the best edge, $\left( \mathbf{v}_{\rm min}, \mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)$. An edge is added to the spanning tree if and only if

1.  an *estimate* of its cost *could* provide a better *solution*, given the current tree (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln"}),[]{#item:edge:estBetterSoln label="item:edge:estBetterSoln"} $$\begin{align*}
            g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) < c_{i},%
    \end{align*}$$

2.  an *estimate* of its cost *could* improve the *current tree* (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph\]](#algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph"}),[]{#item:edge:estBetterGraph label="item:edge:estBetterGraph"} $$\begin{align*}
            g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) < g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right),%
    \end{align*}$$

3.  its *real* cost *could* provide a better *solution*, given the current tree (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln"}),[]{#item:edge:realBetterSoln label="item:edge:realBetterSoln"} $$\begin{align*}
            g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min}\right) + c\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) < c_{i},%
    \end{align*}$$

4.  and its *real* cost *will* improve the *current tree* (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:improvesGraph\]](#algo:bitstar:improvesGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:improvesGraph"}),[]{#item:edge:realBetterGraph label="item:edge:realBetterGraph"} $$\begin{align*}
            g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min}\right) + c\left(\mathbf{v}_{\rm min},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) < g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right).%
    \end{align*}$$

For collision-free edges, Conditions [\[item:edge:estBetterSoln\]](#item:edge:estBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterSoln"} and [\[item:edge:realBetterSoln\]](#item:edge:realBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:realBetterSoln"} are always true in the absence of a solution, while Conditions [\[item:edge:estBetterGraph\]](#item:edge:estBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterGraph"} and [\[item:edge:realBetterGraph\]](#item:edge:realBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:realBetterGraph"} are always true when the target of the edge, $\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}$, is not in the spanning tree.

Checking if the edge could ever provide a better solution or improve the current tree (Conditions [\[item:edge:estBetterSoln\]](#item:edge:estBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterSoln"} and [\[item:edge:estBetterGraph\]](#item:edge:estBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterGraph"}) allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to reject edges without calculating their true cost (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canBeBetterSoln"}--[\[algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph\]](#algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canBeBetterGraph"}). Condition [\[item:edge:estBetterSoln\]](#item:edge:estBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterSoln"} also provides a stopping condition for searching the current [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}. When an edge fails this condition so does the entire queue and both queues can be cleared to start a new batch (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:clearQueue\]](#algo:bitstar:clearQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:clearQueue"}). If the edge fails Condition [\[item:edge:estBetterGraph\]](#item:edge:estBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:estBetterGraph"} it is discarded and the iteration finishes. If the edge passes both these conditions its true cost is calculated by performing a collision check and solving any [2BVPs]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="plural+short"} (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:calculateEdgeCost\]](#algo:bitstar:calculateEdgeCost){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:calculateEdgeCost"}). Note that edges in collision are considered to have infinite cost.

Checking if the real edge could provide a better solution given the current tree (Condition [\[item:edge:realBetterSoln\]](#item:edge:realBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:realBetterSoln"}), allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to reduce tree complexity by rejecting edges that could never improve the current solution (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:canRealEdgeBeBetterSoln"}). Checking if the real edge improves the current tree (Condition [\[item:edge:realBetterGraph\]](#item:edge:realBetterGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="item:edge:realBetterGraph"}), assures the cost-to-come of the explicit tree decreases monotonically (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:improvesGraph\]](#algo:bitstar:improvesGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:improvesGraph"}). If the edge fails either of these conditions it is discarded and the iteration finishes.

An edge passing all of these conditions is added to the spanning tree. If the target vertex is already connected (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:isInTree\]](#algo:bitstar:isInTree){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:isInTree"}), then the edge represents a *rewiring* and the current edge must be removed (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:findParent\]](#algo:bitstar:findParent){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:findParent"}--[\[algo:bitstar:rmParent\]](#algo:bitstar:rmParent){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:rmParent"}). Otherwise, the edge represents an *expansion* of the tree and the target vertex must be moved from the set of unconnected states to the set of vertices, inserted into the vertex queue for future expansion, and marked as a never-expanded vertex (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:rmSample\]](#algo:bitstar:rmSample){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:rmSample"}--[\[algo:bitstar:addVertex\]](#algo:bitstar:addVertex){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:addVertex"}). The new vertex is also added to the set of vertices in the goal region if appropriate (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Lines [\[algo:bitstar:isSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:isSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:isSoln"}--[\[algo:bitstar:addSoln\]](#algo:bitstar:addSoln){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:addSoln"}).

The new edge is then finally added to the tree (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:addEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:addEdge"}) and the current best solution is updated as necessary (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:cmin\]](#algo:bitstar:cmin){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:cmin"}). The search then continues by selecting the next edge in the queue (Section [3.4](#sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:getEdge"}) or increasing the approximation accuracy if the current [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} cannot provide a better solution (Section [3.3](#sec:bitstar:algo:batch){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:batch"}).

## Vertex Expansion (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue\]](#algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:updateEdgeQueue"}; Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:expandVertex}

The function $\mathtt{ExpandNextVertex}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{V},\mathcal{Q}_{E},c_{i}\right)$ removes the front of the vertex queue (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:popVertexQueue\]](#algo:expand:popVertexQueue){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:popVertexQueue"}) and adds its outgoing edges in the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} to the edge queue. The [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} is defined using the results of @karaman_ijrr11 to limit graph complexity while maintaining almost-sure asymptotic convergence to the optimum. Edges exist between a vertex and the $k_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}$-closest states or all states within a distance of $r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}$, with $$\begin{align}
    r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}&> r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}^*,\nonumber\\
    r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}^*&\coloneqq \left(2 \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)
                                \left(\frac{\min\left\lbrace\lambda\left(X\right),\lambda\left(X_{\widehat{f}}\right)\right\rbrace}{\zeta_{n}}\right)\nonumber
                                \vphantom{\left(\frac{\log\left(\left|V\right|+\left|X_{\rm unconn}\right| - m\right)}{\left|V\right|+\left|X_{\rm unconn}\right|- m}\right)}\right.\\
                                &\hspace{40pt}
                                \left.\vphantom{2 \left(1 + \frac{1}{n}\right)
                                                \left(\frac{\min\left\lbrace\lambda\left(X\right),\lambda\left(X_{\widehat{f}}\right)\right\rbrace}{\zeta_{n}}\right)}
                                \left(\frac{\log\left(\left|V\right|+\left|X_{\rm unconn}\right| - m\right)}{\left|V\right|+\left|X_{\rm unconn}\right|- m}\right)
                            \right)^{\frac{1}{n}},\label{eqn:radius:bitstar}
\end{align}$$ and $$\begin{align}
    k_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}&> k_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}^*,\nonumber\\
    k_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}^*&\coloneqq e\left(1+\frac{1}{n}\right)\log\left(\left|V\right|+\left|X_{\rm unconn}\right| - m\right),\label{eqn:knearest:bitstar}
\end{align}$$ where $\left|\cdot\right|$ is the cardinality of a set, $m$ is the number of samples added in the last batch, $\lambda\left(\cdot\right)$ is the Lebesgue measure of a set (e.g., the *volume*), and $\zeta_{n}$ is the Lebesgue measure of an $n$-dimensional unit ball. Recent work has presented different expressions [@janson_ijrr15] and expressions for non-Euclidean spaces [@kleinbort_wafr16].

This connection limit is calculated from the cardinality of the graph *minus* the $m$ new samples to simplify proving almost-sure asymptotic optimality (Section [4](#sec:anal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:anal"}). This lower bound will be large for the initial sparse batches but it can be thresholded with a maximum edge length, as is done by [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, i.e., $r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}' \coloneqq \min\left\lbrace r_{\mathrm{max}}, r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}\right\rbrace$. The function $\mathtt{Near}$ returns the states that meet the selected [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} connection criteria for a given vertex.

Every vertex in the tree is either expanded or pruned in every batch. Processing all the outgoing edges from vertices would result in [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} repeatedly considering the same previously rejected edges. This can be avoided by using the sets of never-expanded vertices, $V_{\rm unexpnd}$, and new samples, $X_{\rm new}$, to add only *previously unconsidered* edges to the edge queue.

Whether edges to unconnected samples are new depends on whether the source vertex has previously been expanded (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:newForSamples\]](#algo:expand:newForSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:newForSamples"}). If it has not been expanded then none of its outgoing connections have been considered and all nearby unconnected samples are potential descendants (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:nearAllSamples\]](#algo:expand:nearAllSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:nearAllSamples"}). If it has been expanded then any connections to 'old' unconnected samples have already been considered and rejected and only the 'new' samples are considered as potential descendants (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:nearNewSamples\]](#algo:expand:nearNewSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:nearNewSamples"}). The subset of these potential edges that could improve the current solution are added to the queue in both situations (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:insertNearSamples\]](#algo:expand:insertNearSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:insertNearSamples"}).

Whether edges to connected samples (i.e., *rewirings*) are new also depends on whether the source vertex has been expanded (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:newForVertices\]](#algo:expand:newForVertices){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:newForVertices"}). If it has not been expanded then all nearby connected vertices are considered as potential descendants (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:nearVertices\]](#algo:expand:nearVertices){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:nearVertices"}). The subset of these potential edges that could improve the current solution *and* the current tree are added to the queue (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:insertRewire\]](#algo:expand:insertRewire){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:insertRewire"}) and the vertex is then marked as expanded (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:markOld\]](#algo:expand:markOld){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:markOld"}).

If a vertex has previously been expanded then no rewirings are considered. Improvements in the tree may now allow a previously considered edge to improve connected vertices but considering these connections would require repeatedly reconsidering infeasible edges. As in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, this lack of *propagated rewiring* has no effect on almost-sure asymptotic optimality.

## Graph Pruning (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:prune\]](#algo:bitstar:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:prune"}; Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}) {#sec:bitstar:algo:prune}

The function, $\mathtt{Prune}\left(\mathcal{T},X_{\rm unconn},c_{i}\right)$, reduces the complexity of both the approximation of the continuous planning problem (i.e., the implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}) and its search (i.e., the explicit spanning tree) by limiting them to the informed set. It removes any states that can *never* provide a better solution and disconnects any vertices that cannot provide a better solution *given the current tree*. Disconnected vertices that *could* improve the solution with a better connection are reused as new samples in the next batch to maintain uniform sample density in the informed set. This assures that every vertex is either expanded or pruned in each batch as assumed by $\mathtt{ExpandNextVertex}$ to avoid reconsidering edges (Section [3.6](#sec:bitstar:algo:expandVertex){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar:algo:expandVertex"}).

The set of recycled vertices is initialized as an empty set (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:resetReuse\]](#algo:prune:resetReuse){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:resetReuse"}) and all unconnected states that cannot provide a better solution (i.e., are not members of the informed set) are removed (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:pruneSamples\]](#algo:prune:pruneSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:pruneSamples"}). The connected vertices are then incrementally pruned in order of increasing cost-to-come (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:iterateGraph\]](#algo:prune:iterateGraph){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:iterateGraph"}) by identifying those that can never provide a better solution or improve the solution given the *current* tree (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:pruneCond\]](#algo:prune:pruneCond){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:pruneCond"}). Vertices that fail either condition are removed from the tree by disconnecting their incoming edge and removing them from the vertex set and any labelling sets (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Lines [\[algo:prune:rmVertex\]](#algo:prune:rmVertex){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:rmVertex"}--[\[algo:prune:rmEdge\]](#algo:prune:rmEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:rmEdge"}).

Any disconnected vertex that could provide a better solution (i.e., is a member of the informed set) is reused as a sample in the next batch (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Lines [\[algo:prune:reuseCond\]](#algo:prune:reuseCond){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:reuseCond"}--[\[algo:prune:addReuse\]](#algo:prune:addReuse){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:addReuse"}). This maintains uniform sample density in the informed set and assures that vertices will be reconnected if future improvements allow them to provide a better solution. This set of disconnected vertices is returned to [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} at the end of the pruning procedure (Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:returnReuse\]](#algo:prune:returnReuse){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:returnReuse"}).

## Practical Considerations {#sec:bitstar:algo:pract}

Algs. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}--[\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"} describe a generic version of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and leave room for a number of practical improvements depending on the specific implementation.

Searches (e.g., Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}, Line [\[algo:expand:nearAllSamples\]](#algo:expand:nearAllSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand:nearAllSamples"}) can be implemented efficiently with appropriate datastructures that do not require an exhaustive global search [e.g., $k$-d trees or randomly transformed grids; @kleinbort_icra15]. Pruning (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:prune\]](#algo:bitstar:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:prune"}; Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}) is computationally expensive and should only occur when a new solution has been found or limited to *significant* changes in solution cost.

In an object-oriented programming language, many of the sets (e.g., $X_{\rm new}$, $V_{\rm unexpnd}$) can be implemented more efficiently as labels. The cost-to-come to a state in the current tree, $g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\cdot\right)$, can also be implemented efficiently using back pointers.

While queues can be implemented efficiently by using containers that sort on insertion, the value of elements in the vertex and edge queues will change when vertices are rewired. There appears to be little practical difference between efficiently resorting the affected elements in these queues and only lazily resorting the queue before finishing to assure no elements have been missed.

Depending on the datastructure used for the edge queue, it may be beneficial to remove unnecessary entries when a new edge is added to the spanning tree, i.e., by adding $$\begin{align*}
%
    \mathcal{Q}_{E}\xleftarrow{\scriptscriptstyle -}&\left\lbrace \left( \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)\in\mathcal{Q}_{E}\;\;\middle|\;\; \widehat{g}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)
                                \vphantom{+ \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) \geq g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)}\right.\\
                               &\hspace{80pt}
                                \left.\vphantom{\left( \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)\in\mathcal{Q}_{E}\widehat{g}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)}
                          {} + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{v},\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right) \geq g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\rm min}\right)\right\rbrace
\end{align*}$$ after Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:addEdge\]](#algo:bitstar:addEdge){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:addEdge"}.

# Analysis {#sec:anal}

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} performance is analyzed theoretically using the results of @karaman_ijrr11. It is shown to be probabilistically complete (Theorem [2](#thm:pc){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:pc"}) and almost-surely asymptotically optimal (Theorem [3](#thm:asao){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:asao"}). Its search ordering is also shown to be equivalent to a lazy version of the ordering used in [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} (Lemma [4](#lem:lpa){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:lpa"}).

::: {#thm:pc .thm}
**Theorem 2** (Probabilistic completeness of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}). *The probability that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds a feasible solution to a given path planning problem, if one exists, when given infinite samples is one, $$\begin{align*}
%
        \liminf_{q\to \infty}P&\left(\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\in\Sigma,\, \sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\left(0\right) = \mathbf{x}_{\rm start},
                                                        \vphantom{\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\left(1\right) \in X_{\rm goal}}\right.\\
                                                        &\hspace{1in}\left.\vphantom{\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\in\Sigma,\, \sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\left(0\right) = \mathbf{x}_{\rm start},}
                                                    \sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\left(1\right) \in X_{\rm goal}\right) = 1,
\end{align*}$$ where $q$ is the number of samples, $\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}$ is the path found by [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} from those samples, and $\Sigma$ is the set of all feasible, collision-free paths.*
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Proof of Theorem [2](#thm:pc){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:pc"} follows from the proof of almost-sure asymptotic optimality (Theorem [3](#thm:asao){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:asao"}). ◻
:::

::: {#thm:asao .thm}
**Theorem 3** (Almost-sure asymptotic optimality of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}). *The probability that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} converges asymptotically towards the optimal solution of a given path planning problem, if one exists, when given infinite samples is one, $$\begin{align*}
        P\left(\limsup_{q\to \infty}c\left(\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}\right) = c\left(\sigma^{*}\right)\right) = 1,
\end{align*}$$ where $q$ is the number of samples, $\sigma_{q,\mathrm{BIT}^*}$ is the path found by [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} from $q$ samples and $\sigma^{*}$ is optimal solution to the planning problem.*
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* Theorem [3](#thm:asao){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:asao"} is proven by showing that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} considers at least the same edges as [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} for a sequence of states, $X_{\rm samples}= \left(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_q\right)$, and connection limit, $r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}\geq r_{\mathrm{RRT}^*}$.

[RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} incrementally builds a tree from a sequence of samples. For each state in the sequence, $\mathbf{x}_k\in X_{\rm samples}$, it considers the neighbourhood of earlier states that are within the connection limit, $$\begin{align*}
%
        X_{{\rm near},k}\coloneqq &\left\lbrace\mathbf{x}_j\in X_{\rm samples}\;\; \middle| \;\; j< k,
                                \vphantom{\left\| \mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}_j \right\|_{2} \leq r_{\mathrm{RRT}^*}}\right.\\
                                &\left.\vphantom{\mathbf{x}_j\in X_{\rm samples}j< k,}
                            \hspace{1.2in}\left\| \mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}_j \right\|_{2} \leq r_{\mathrm{RRT}^*}\right\rbrace.
\end{align*}$$ It selects the connection from this neighbourhood that minimizes the cost-to-come of the state and then evaluates the ability of connections from this state to reduce the cost-to-come of the other states in the neighbourhood.

Given the same sequence of states, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} groups them into batches of samples, $X_{\rm samples}= \left(Y_1, Y_2, \ldots, Y_\ell\right)$, where each batch is a set of $m< q$ samples, e.g., $Y_1 \coloneqq \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_2,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_m\right\rbrace$. It incrementally builds a tree by processing this batched sequence of samples. For each state in the sequence, $\mathbf{y}\in Y_k$, it considers the neighbourhood of states from the same or earlier batches within the connection limit, $$\begin{align*}
        X_{{\rm near},k}\coloneqq \left\lbrace \mathbf{x}\in Y_j\;\;\middle|\;\;j\leq k,\;\left\| \mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x} \right\|_{2} \leq r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}\right\rbrace.
\end{align*}$$ It adds the edge in this neighbourhood to the tree that minimizes the cost-to-come of the state and considers all the outgoing edges to connect its neighbours. This set of edges contains all those considered by [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} for an equivalent connection limit, $r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}\geq r_{\mathrm{RRT}^*}$, given that $m\geq 1$.

As [\[eqn:radius:bitstar\]](#eqn:radius:bitstar){reference-type="eqref" reference="eqn:radius:bitstar"} uses the same connection radius for a batch that [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} would use for the first sample in the batch and the connection radius of both are monotonically decreasing, this shows that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} considers at least the same edges as [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. From these edges, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} selects those that improve the cost-to-come of the target state and could currently provide a better solution as in @karaman_icra11. It is therefore almost-surely asymptotically optimal as stated in Theorem [3](#thm:asao){reference-type="ref" reference="thm:asao"}. ◻
:::

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} searches the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} in order of potential solution quality using an edge queue. This is shown to be equivalent to the vertex queue ordering used by [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}/[TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} with a *lazy* approximation of incoming edge costs (Lemma [4](#lem:lpa){reference-type="ref" reference="lem:lpa"}). The search itself is not equivalent to [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} as [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} does not reconsider outgoing connections from rewired vertices (i.e., it does not propagate rewirings). It is instead a version of [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

::: {#lem:lpa .lem}
**Lemma 4** (The equivalent queue ordering of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} and [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}/[TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}). *The edge ordering in [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} that uses first the sum of a vertex's estimated cost-to-go, estimated incoming edge cost, and current cost-to-come of its parent, $$\begin{align*}
        g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{u}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right) + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{v}\right),
\end{align*}$$ then the estimated cost-to-come of the vertex, $$\begin{align*}
        g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{u}\right) + \widehat{c}\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right),
\end{align*}$$ and then the cost-to-come of its parent, $$\begin{align*}
        g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{u}\right),
\end{align*}$$ is equivalent to the vertex ordering in [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} [@koenig_ai04] and [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} [@aine_ai16].*
:::

::: proof
*Proof.* [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} use a queue of vertices ordered lexicographically first on the solution cost constrained to go through the vertex and then the cost-to-come of the vertex. Both these terms are calculated for a vertex, $\mathbf{v}\in V$, considering all its possible incoming edges (referred to as the *rhs-value* in [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}), i.e., $$\begin{align*}
    \min\limits_{\left( \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\right)\in E}\left\lbrace g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)+c\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right)\right\rbrace + \widehat{h}\left(\mathbf{v}\right)\\
    \shortintertext{and}
    \min\limits_{\left( \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}\right)\in E}\left\lbrace g_{\mathcal{T}}\left(\mathbf{u}\right)+c\left(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\right)\right\rbrace.
\end{align*}$$

This minimum requires calculating the true edge cost between a vertex and all of its possible parents. This calculation is expensive in sampling-based planning (e.g., collision checking, differential constraints, etc.) and reducing its calculation is desirable. This can be achieved by incrementally calculating the minimum in the order given by an admissible heuristic estimate of edge cost. Considering edges into the vertex in order of increasing *estimated* cost calculates a running minimum that can be stopped when the estimated cost through the next edge to consider is higher than the current minimum.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} combines the minimum calculations for individual vertices into a single edge queue. This simultaneously calculates the minimum cost-to-come for each vertex in the current graph while expanding vertices in order of increasing estimated solution cost. ◻
:::

# Modifications and Extensions {#sec:mods}

The basic version of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} presented in Algs. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}--[\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"} can be modified and extended to include features that may improve performance for some planning applications. Section [5.1](#sec:mods:delay){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods:delay"} presents a method to delay rewiring the tree until an initial solution is found. This prioritizes exploring the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} to find solutions and may be beneficial in time-constrained applications. Section [5.2](#sec:mods:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods:jit"} presents a method to delay sampling until necessary. This avoids approximating regions of the planning problem that are never searched, improves performance in large planning problems, and avoids the need to define *a priori* limits in unbounded problems.

Section [5.3](#sec:mods:drop){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods:drop"} presents a method for [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to occasionally remove unconnected samples while maintaining almost-sure asymptotic optimality. This avoids repeated connection attempts to infeasible states and may be beneficial in problems where many regions of the free space are unreachable.

Section [5.4](#sec:mods:sorrtstar){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods:sorrtstar"} extends the idea of reducing the number of connections attempted per sample during an ordered search to develop [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. This version of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} uses batches of samples to order its search of the problem domain by potential solution quality, as in [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, but uses a `steer` function and only makes one connection attempt per sample, as in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

## Delayed Rewiring {#sec:mods:delay}

Many robotic systems have a finite amount of computational time available to solve planning problems. In these situations, rewiring the existing tree before an initial solution is found reduces the likelihood of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} solving the given problem. A method to delay rewirings until a solution is found is presented in Alg. [\[algo:delay\]](#algo:delay){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay"} as simple modifications to $\mathtt{ExpandNextVertex}$, with changes highlighted in red (cf. Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}). The rewirings are still performed once a solution is found and this method does not affect almost-sure asymptotic optimality.

Rewirings are delayed by separately tracking whether vertices are unexpanded to nearby unconnected samples, $V_{\rm unexpnd}$, and unexpanded to nearby connected vertices, $V_{\rm delayed}$. This allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to prioritize finding a solution by only considering edges to new samples until a solution is found and then improving the graph by considering rewirings from the existing vertices.

A vertex is moved from the never-expanded set to the delayed set when edges to its potential unconnected descendants are added to the edge queue (Alg. [\[algo:delay\]](#algo:delay){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay"}, Lines [\[algo:delay:markOld\]](#algo:delay:markOld){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay:markOld"}--[\[algo:delay:markDelayed\]](#algo:delay:markDelayed){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay:markDelayed"}). This allows future expansions of the vertex to avoid old unconnected samples while tracking that the vertex's outgoing rewirings have not yet been considered. Vertices in the delayed set are expanded as potential rewirings of other connected vertices once a solution is found (Alg. [\[algo:delay\]](#algo:delay){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay"}, Line [\[algo:delay:newForVertices\]](#algo:delay:newForVertices){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay:newForVertices"}) and the delayed label is removed (Alg. [\[algo:delay\]](#algo:delay){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay"}, Line [\[algo:delay:markExpanded\]](#algo:delay:markExpanded){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:delay:markExpanded"}).

This extension requires initializing and resetting $V_{\rm delayed}$ along with the other labelling sets (e.g., Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:initLabels\]](#algo:bitstar:initLabels){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:initLabels"} and Alg. [\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"}, Line [\[algo:prune:rmVertex\]](#algo:prune:rmVertex){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune:rmVertex"}). This extension is included in the publicly available [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"} implementation of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

## Just-in-Time ([JIT]{acronym-label="JIT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}) Sampling {#sec:mods:jit}

Many robotic systems operate in environments that are unbounded (e.g., the outdoors). These problems have commonly required using *a priori* search limits to make the problem domain tractable. Selecting these limits can be difficult and may prevent finding a solution (e.g., defining a domain that does not contain a solution) or reduce performance (e.g., defining a domain too large to search sufficiently). A method to avoid these problems in [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} by generating samples *just in time* ([JIT]{acronym-label="JIT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}) is presented in Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"} and accompanying modifications to the main algorithm. This modification generates samples only when needed by [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}'s search while still maintaining uniform sample density and almost-sure asymptotic convergence to the optimum. This avoids approximating regions of the problem that are not used to find a solution and allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to operate directly on large or unbounded planning problems.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} searches a planning problem by constructing and searching an implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} defined by a number of uniformly distributed samples (vertices) and their relative distances (edges). These samples are given explicitly in Algs. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}--[\[algo:prune\]](#algo:prune){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:prune"} but are not used until they could be a descendant of a vertex in the tree. This occurs when the samples are within the local neighbourhood of the vertex (Fig. [5](#fig:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:jit"}). The cost associated with the informed set containing the neighbourhood of a vertex, $X_{\rm near}$, for planning problems seeking to minimize path length, $$\begin{align*}
%
    c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\coloneqq \max_{\mathbf{x}\in X_{\rm near}}\left\lbrace \widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\rbrace,
\end{align*}$$ is bounded from above as, $$\begin{align*}
%
    c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\leq \widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + 2r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*}.
\end{align*}$$

[JIT]{acronym-label="JIT" acronym-form="singular+short"} sampling only generates samples when necessary to expand vertices by incrementally sampling this growing informed set. It does this while maintaining uniform sample density by tracking the previously sampled size of the set, $c_{\rm sampled}$, and only generating the new samples necessary to increase it. The function $\mathtt{UpdateSamples}\left(\mathbf{v}, c_{\rm sampled}, c_{i}\right)$ generates [JIT]{acronym-label="JIT" acronym-form="singular+short"} samples for vertex expansion in problems seeking to minimize path length (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}). The required size of the sampled informed set, $c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}$, is a function of the neighbourhood and the maximum size of the informed set (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}, Line [\[algo:jit:reqCost\]](#algo:jit:reqCost){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:reqCost"}). If it is higher than the previously sampled cost, $c_{\rm sampled}$, then the local neighbourhood has not been completely sampled and new samples are generated (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}, Line [\[algo:jit:isNotSampled\]](#algo:jit:isNotSampled){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:isNotSampled"}). If it is lower then the neighbourhood has already been sampled and no new samples are generated.

::: algorithm
$c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\gets \min{\left\lbrace\widehat{f}\left(\mathbf{v}\right) + 2r_{\mathrm{BIT}^*},c_{i}\right\rbrace}$[]{#algo:jit:reqCost label="algo:jit:reqCost"} $\lambda_{\rm sample}\gets \lambda_{\rm PHS}\left(c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\right) - \lambda_{\rm PHS}\left(c_{\rm sampled}\right)$[]{#algo:jit:reqVol label="algo:jit:reqVol"} $m' \gets \rho\lambda_{\rm sample}$[]{#algo:jit:reqNum label="algo:jit:reqNum"} $X_{\rm new}\xleftarrow{\scriptscriptstyle +}\mathtt{Sample}\left(m', c_{\rm sampled}, c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\right)$[]{#algo:jit:sample label="algo:jit:sample"} $X_{\rm unconn}\xleftarrow{\scriptscriptstyle +}X_{\rm new}$[]{#algo:jit:add label="algo:jit:add"} $c_{\rm sampled}\gets c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}$[]{#algo:jit:markSampled label="algo:jit:markSampled"}
:::

The number of required new samples, $m'$, can be calculated from the chosen batch sample density, $\rho$, and the volume of the shell being sampled (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}, Lines [\[algo:jit:reqVol\]](#algo:jit:reqVol){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:reqVol"}--[\[algo:jit:reqNum\]](#algo:jit:reqNum){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:reqNum"}). These samples are added to the set of new states and the set of unconnected states (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}, Lines [\[algo:jit:sample\]](#algo:jit:sample){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:sample"}--[\[algo:jit:add\]](#algo:jit:add){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:add"}). Finally, the sampled cost is updated to reflect the new size of the sampled informed set (Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"}, Line [\[algo:jit:markSampled\]](#algo:jit:markSampled){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit:markSampled"}).

The function $\lambda_{\rm PHS}\left(\cdot\right)$ calculates the measure of the prolate hyperspheroid defined by the start and goal with the given cost, $$\begin{align*}
    \lambda\left(X_{\widehat{f}}\right) \leq \lambda\left(X_{\rm PHS}\right) = \frac{c_{i}\left( c_{i}^2 - c_{\rm min}^2 \right)^{\frac{n-1}{2}} \zeta_{n}}{2^n},
\end{align*}$$ where $\zeta_{n}$ is the Lebesgue measure of an $n$-dimensional unit ball, $$\begin{align*}
    \zeta_{n} \coloneqq \frac{\pi^{\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(\frac{n}{2} + 1\right)},
\end{align*}$$ and $\Gamma\left(\cdot\right)$ is the gamma function, an extension of factorials to real numbers [@gamma_function]. The function $\mathtt{Sample}\left(m',c_{\rm sampled},c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\right)$ generates informed samples within the cost interval $\left[c_{\rm sampled},c_{\mbox{\rm\scriptsize req'd}}\right)$ and may be implemented with rejection sampling.

Using Alg. [\[algo:jit\]](#algo:jit){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:jit"} requires modifying Algs. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"} and [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}. The $\mathtt{Sample}$ function of a batch (Alg. [\[algo:bitstar\]](#algo:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar"}, Line [\[algo:bitstar:sample\]](#algo:bitstar:sample){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:bitstar:sample"}) is replaced with an initialization of the sampled cost variable, $c_{\rm sampled}\gets0$, and $\mathtt{UpdateSamples}\left(\mathbf{v}, c_{\rm sampled}, c_{i}\right)$ is added to the front of $\mathtt{ExpandNextVertex}$ (Alg. [\[algo:expand\]](#algo:expand){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:expand"}). This extension is included in the publicly available [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"} implementation of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

## Sample Removal {#sec:mods:drop}

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} approximates continuous planning problems with an implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}. It efficiently increases the accuracy of this approximation by focusing it to the informed set. It alternately increases density by adding new samples and shrinks the set by searching the existing samples for better solutions. This builds an explicit spanning tree that contains all states that could *currently* provide a better solution but may not use every state in the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

States in an informed set may not be able to improve a solution for many reasons. The approximation may be insufficiently accurate (i.e., low sample density) to capture difficult features (e.g., narrow passages) or represent sufficiently optimal paths. The informed set may also include regions of the problem domain that cannot improve the solution due to unconsidered problem features (e.g., barriers separating free space) or because it is otherwise poorly chosen (i.e., low precision). Unconnected samples in the first situation may later be beneficial to the search but samples in the second represent unnecessary computational cost. Periodically removing these samples would reduce the complexity of the implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} and avoid repeatedly attempting to connect them to new vertices in the tree.

Unconnected samples can be removed while maintaining the requirements for almost-sure asymptotic optimality by modifying the [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} connection limits to consider only uniformly distributed samples. This can be accomplished by using the number of uniformly distributed samples added since the last removal of unconnected states in [\[eqn:radius:bitstar\]](#eqn:radius:bitstar){reference-type="eqref" reference="eqn:radius:bitstar"} and [\[eqn:knearest:bitstar\]](#eqn:knearest:bitstar){reference-type="eqref" reference="eqn:knearest:bitstar"}. This simple extension is included in the publicly available [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"} implementation of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

Approaching sampling-based planning as the search of an implicit [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} motivates [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to consider multiple connections to each sample. Section [5.3](#sec:mods:drop){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods:drop"} presents a method to limit this number of attempts by periodically removing samples. The natural extension of this idea is to consider only a single connection attempt per sample, as in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. This motivates the development of [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, a version of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} that orders its search by potential solution quality by sorting batches of samples.

[SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is presented in Alg. [\[algo:sorrtstar\]](#algo:sorrtstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar"} as simple modifications of Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}, with changes highlighted in red [cf. @gammell_iros14; @gammell_tro18]. Instead of expanding the tree towards a randomly generated sample at each iteration, [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} extends the tree towards the best unconsidered sample in its current batch. It accomplishes this by using a queue of samples, $\mathcal{Q}_{\rm Samples}$, ordered by potential solution cost, $\widehat{f}\left(\cdot\right)$. This queue is filled with $m$ samples (Alg. [\[algo:sorrtstar\]](#algo:sorrtstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar"}, Lines [\[algo:sorrtstar:checkEmpty\]](#algo:sorrtstar:checkEmpty){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar:checkEmpty"}--[\[algo:sorrtstar:addSamples\]](#algo:sorrtstar:addSamples){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar:addSamples"}) and the search proceeds by expanding the tree towards the best sample in the queue (Alg. [\[algo:sorrtstar\]](#algo:sorrtstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar"}, Line [\[algo:sorrtstar:popSample\]](#algo:sorrtstar:popSample){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar:popSample"}). This orders the search for the $m$ samples in a batch, at which point a new batch of samples is generated and the search continues.

The function $\mathtt{PopBestInQueue}\left(\cdot\right)$ pops the best element off the front of a queue given its ordering. A goal bias may be implemented in [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} by adding a small probability of sampling the goal instead of removing the best sample from the queue. This algorithm is publicly available in [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

[SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} can be viewed as a simplified version of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} that only considers the best-possible edges. Attempting to connect each sample once avoids the computational cost of repeated connection attempts to infeasible samples but still maintains some dependence on the sampling order. High-utility samples (e.g., samples near the optimum) may be underutilized depending on the state of the tree when they are found. This can become problematic if these samples have a low sampling probability (e.g., samples in narrow passages). Making multiple connections attempts per sample and retaining samples for multiple batches allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to exploit these useful samples more than algorithms such as [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. As seen in Section [6](#sec:exp){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp"}, this results in different performance, especially in high state dimensions.

# Experiments {#sec:exp}

The benefits of ordering the search of continuous planning problems are demonstrated on simulated problems in $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbb{R}^4$, $\mathbb{R}^8$, and $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ (Section [6.1](#sec:exp:sim){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp:sim"}) and one- and two-armed problems for [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+short"} (Section [6.2](#sec:exp:herb){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp:herb"}) using [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"}. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is compared to the [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"} versions of [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"}, [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect, [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ (i.e., [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^X^ with $\epsilon = 0$), [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}, and [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

[SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} both used $m=100$ samples per batch for all problems. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} also thresholded its initial connection radius by using the same radius for both the first and second batches.

### Dual-Enclosure Problems {#sec:exp:sim:bug}

The algorithms were tested on problems with two enclosures in $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbb{R}^4$, $\mathbb{R}^8$, and $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ (Fig. [6](#fig:exp:defn){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:defn"}a). The problems consisted of a (hyper)cube of width $l=2.8$ with the start and goal located at $\left[-0.5,0,\ldots,0\right]^T$ and $\left[0.5,0,\ldots,0\right]^T$, respectively. The enclosures were symmetric around the start and goal with a thickness of $t=0.1$ and openings of width $b=0.6$. As all almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners struggled to solve the problem in $\mathbb{R}^{16}$, it was run for $1000$ seconds with recording and interpolation periods of $2\times10^{-3}$ seconds.

The results are presented in Fig. [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"} with the percent of trials solved and the median solution cost plotted versus run time. The results demonstrate the advantages of ordering the approximation and search of difficult planning problems. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is competitive to other almost-surely asymptotically optimal planning algorithms in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$ and outperforms all algorithms other than [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect in higher state dimensions. In all dimensions, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds a solution in every trial (i.e., attains $100\%$ success) sooner than the other anytime almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners.

Specifically in $\mathbb{R}^2$, the median time required for [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to find an initial solution is more than that of the [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-based planners (Fig. [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}c); however, once a solution is found, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds better or equivalent solutions than the best performing [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} planners at any given time. [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} slightly outperforms the other almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners but is not anytime.

The performance of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-based planners decreases more rapidly with increasing state dimension on this problem than planners that process multiple samples such as [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} outperforms all planners other than [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect in terms of success rate and median solution cost in $\mathbb{R}^{4}$ (Figs. [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}b and [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}d). This difference increases in $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ where the [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}-based planners only find a solution in the available time in $35\%$ of the trials or less (Figs. [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}e and [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}g). All almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners struggle to solve the problem in $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ but [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is the only one that finds a solution in more than $50\%$ of the trials (Figs. [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}f and [7](#fig:exp:bugtrap){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:bugtrap"}h).

### Problems with Many Homotopy Classes {#sec:exp:sim:reg}

The algorithms were tested on problems with many homotopy classes in $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbb{R}^4$, $\mathbb{R}^8$, and $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ (Fig. [6](#fig:exp:defn){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:defn"}b). The problems consisted of a (hyper)cube of width $l=4$ with the start and goal located at $\left[-0.5,0,\ldots,0\right]^T$ and $\left[0.5,0,\ldots,0\right]^T$, respectively. The problem domain was filled with a regular pattern of axis-aligned (hyper)rectangular obstacles with a width such that the start and goal were $5$ 'columns' apart.

The results are presented in Fig. [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"} with the percent of trials solved and the median solution cost plotted versus run time. These results demonstrate both the advantages and disadvantages of attempting multiple connections per sample in problems with many disconnected obstacles. In lower dimensions, informed algorithms that only make a single connection attempt per sample (e.g., Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}) have better median solution costs at any given time. In higher dimensions, only the bidirectional [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect and algorithms that make multiple connection attempts per sample (e.g., [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}) find solutions in a reasonable amount of time. In all dimensions, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds a solution in every trial (i.e., attains $100\%$ success) sooner than every planner tested other than [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect.

Specifically, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is the most likely almost-surely asymptotically optimal planning algorithm to solve the problem in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$, $\mathbb{R}^{4}$, and $\mathbb{R}^{8}$ (Figs. [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}a, [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}b, and [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}e) but the informed, [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-based algorithms have better median solution costs (Figs. [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}c, [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}d, [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}g). This advantage disappears in $\mathbb{R}^{16}$ where [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is the only anytime almost-surely asymptotically optimal planner to always solve the planning problem in the given time (Figs. [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}f and [8](#fig:exp:regular){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:regular"}h).

This difference in performance may arise from the relative difficulty of planning tasks in different state dimensions. It appears that in lower state dimensions the main challenge of this problem is avoiding obstacles. In these situations, informed, [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}-based algorithms will outperform [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} as they only make one connection attempt per sample. In higher state dimensions, it appears that the exponential increase in problem measure (i.e., the curse of dimensionality) makes navigating towards the goal an equal challenge and [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} outperforms these other algorithms by considering multiple connection attempts per sample in an ordered fashion.

### Random Problems {#sec:exp:sim:rand}

The planners were tested on randomly generated problems in $\mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbb{R}^4$, $\mathbb{R}^8$, and $\mathbb{R}^{16}$. The worlds consisted of a (hyper)cube of width $l=2$ populated with approximately $75$ random axis-aligned (hyper)rectangular obstacles that obstruct at most one third of the environment.

For each state dimension, $10$ different random worlds were generated and the planners were tested on each with $100$ different pseudo-random seeds. The true optima for these $10$ problems are different and unknown and there is no meaningful way to compare the results across problems. Results from a representative problem are instead presented in Fig. [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"} with the percent of trials solved and the median solution cost plotted versus computational time.

These experiments show that [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} generally finds better solutions faster than other sampling-based optimal planners and [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"} on these types of problems regardless of the state dimension. It has a higher likelihood of having found a solution at a given computational time (Figs. [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}a, [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}b, [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}e, and [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}f), and converges faster towards the optimum (Figs. [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}c, [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}d, [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}g, and [9](#fig:exp:random){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:random"}h), with the relative improvement increasing with state dimension. The only tested planner that found solutions faster than [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} was [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect, a nonanytime planner that cannot converge to the optimum.

## Path Planning for [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} {#sec:exp:herb}

It is difficult to capture the challenges of actual high-dimensional planning in abstract worlds. Two planning problems inspired by manipulation scenarios were created for [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+short"}, a 14-[DOF]{acronym-label="DOF" acronym-form="singular+short"} mobile manipulation platform.

Start and goal poses were chosen for one arm ($7$ [DOFs]{acronym-label="DOF" acronym-form="plural+short"}, Section [6.2.1](#sec:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp:herb:1arm"}) and two arms ($14$ [DOFs]{acronym-label="DOF" acronym-form="plural+short"}, Section [6.2.2](#sec:exp:herb:2arm){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp:herb:2arm"}) to define planning problems with the objective of minimizing path length through configuration space. They were used to compare the [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"} versions of [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+short"}, [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect, [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ (i.e., [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^X^ with $\epsilon = 0$), Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}, [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}, and [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. The planners were run on each problem $50$ times while recording success rate, initial solution time and cost, and final cost. Trials that did not find a solution were considered to have taken infinite time and have infinite path length, respectively, for the purpose of calculating medians. The number of [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} samples for both problems was chosen to use the majority of the available computational time.

### A One-Armed Planning Problem {#sec:exp:herb:1arm}

A planning problem was defined for [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}'s left arm around a cluttered table (Fig. [10](#fig:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm"}). The arm starts folded at the elbow and held at approximately the level of the table (Figs. [10](#fig:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm"}a and [10](#fig:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm"}b) and must be moved into position to grasp a box (Figs. [10](#fig:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm"}c and [10](#fig:exp:herb:1arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm"}d). The planners were given $20$ seconds of computational time to solve this $7$-[DOF]{acronym-label="DOF" acronym-form="singular+short"} problem with the objective of minimizing path length in configuration space. [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} used $m= 30$ samples.

The percentage of trials that successfully found a solution (Fig. [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}a), the median time and cost of the initial solution (Figs. [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}b and [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}c) and the final cost (Fig. [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}d) were plotted for each planner. Infinite values are not plotted.

The results show [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} finds solutions more often than other almost-sure asymptotically optimal planners on this problem (Fig. [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}a) and also finds better solutions faster than all tested algorithms, including [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-Connect (Figs. [11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}b--[11](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:bars"}d). Fig. [12](#fig:exp:herb:1arm:pic){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:1arm:pic"} presents a composite photograph of [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+short"} executing a path found by [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} for a similar problem.

### A Two-Armed Planning Problem {#sec:exp:herb:2arm}

A second planning problem was defined for both of [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}'s arms moving around a cluttered table (Fig. [13](#fig:exp:herb:2arm){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:2arm"}). The percentage of trials that successfully found a solution (Fig. [14](#fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars"}a), the median time and cost of the initial solution (Figs. [14](#fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars"}b and [14](#fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars"}c) and the final cost (Fig. [14](#fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:exp:herb:2arm:bars"}d) were plotted for each planner. # Discussion & Conclusion {#sec:fin}

Most planning algorithms discretize the search space of continuous path planning problems. Popular approaches in robotics include *a priori* graph- or anytime sampling-based approximations. Both of these approaches are successful but have important limitations.

*A priori* graphs approximate a problem before it is searched. Doing so 'correctly' is challenging since the relationship between resolution and search performance depends on the specific features of a planning problem (e.g., the size and arrangement of obstacles). If the chosen approximation is insufficient (e.g., a sparse graph) it may preclude finding a (suitable) solution but if it is excessive (e.g., a dense graph) it may make finding a solution prohibitively expensive.

Graph-based searches are effective path planning techniques despite these limitations. This is because informed algorithms, such as A\*, use heuristics to order their search by potential solution quality. This not only finds the optimal solution to the given representation (i.e., it is *resolution optimal*) but does so by expanding the minimum number of vertices for the chosen heuristic [i.e., it is *optimally efficient*; @hart_tssc68].

Anytime sampling-based planners alternatively build approximations that increase in resolution. This avoids the need to select a representation *a priori* and allows them to be run indefinitely until a (suitable) solution is found. [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} has a unity probability of finding a solution, if one exists, with an infinite number of samples (i.e., it is *probabilistically complete*) and finds continuously improving solutions [i.e., it is *almost-surely asymptotically optimal*; @karaman_ijrr11].

This has made them effective planning techniques despite the fact their search is often inefficient. Incremental sampling-based planners tend to explore the entire problem domain equally (i.e., they are *space filling*). This wastes computational effort on regions of the problem domain that are not needed to find the solution and can be prohibitively expensive in large planning problems and/or high state dimensions.

Previous attempts to unify these two approaches have been incomplete. They either sacrifice anytime resolution (e.g., [RAstar]{acronym-label="RAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} and [FMTstar]{acronym-label="FMTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}), order the search on metrics other than solution cost and waste computational effort (e.g., [SBAstar]{acronym-label="SBAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}), or do not order all aspects of the problem domain search (e.g., [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^\#^ and [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}^X^). This paper demonstrates that these tradeoffs are unnecessary and that anytime sampling-based planners can be directly and solely ordered by a heuristic estimate of solution cost.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} directly unifies informed graph-based search and sampling-based planning (Section [3](#sec:bitstar){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:bitstar"}). It uses heuristics and batches of random samples to simultaneously build anytime approximations of continuous planning problems and search these approximations in order of potential solution quality. This avoids the computational costs of both searching an improper approximation (graph-based search) and performing an unordered search of the problem domain (sampling-based planning). A version of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is publicly available in [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} approximates the search space by using *batches* of samples to define increasingly dense implicit [RGGs]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="plural+short"}. Building this approximation with batches of *multiple samples* allows each search to be ordered by potential solution quality, as in A\*. Building this approximation from *multiple batches* of samples allows it to be improved indefinitely until it contains a suitable solution, as in [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. As a result, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is probabilistically complete and almost-surely asymptotically optimal (Section [4](#sec:anal){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:anal"}).

This simultaneous approximation and search is done efficiently by using heuristics. The approximation is focused to the regions of the planning problem that could provide better solutions, as in Informed [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}. The denser approximations are searched efficiently by reusing previous information, as in [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. Edge calculations (e.g., [2BVPs]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="plural+short"} and collisions checks) are delayed until necessary, as in *lazy* versions of both graph-based searches and sampling-based planners [e.g., @bohlin_icra00; @sanchez_ijrr02; @helmert_jair06; @branicky_icra01; @cohen_rss14; @hauser_icra15; @salzman_tro16].

A brief set of extensions to [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} are presented (Section [5](#sec:mods){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:mods"}). These include prioritizing an initial solution, avoiding the need to define *a priori* search limits in unbounded problems, and avoiding unreachable areas of the problem domain. These ideas also motivate the development of [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} as an extension of batch-ordered search to the algorithmic simplicity of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} (Alg. [\[algo:sorrtstar\]](#algo:sorrtstar){reference-type="ref" reference="algo:sorrtstar"}). A version of [SORRTstar]{acronym-label="SORRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is publicly available in [OMPL]{acronym-label="OMPL" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

The benefits of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} are demonstrated experimentally on abstract planning problems and simulated experiments for [HERB]{acronym-label="HERB" acronym-form="singular+short"} (Section [6](#sec:exp){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:exp"}). The results highlight the advantages and disadvantages of both using an ordered search and considering multiple connections per sample. As state dimension increases, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} becomes more likely to have found a solution and generally finds better solutions faster than the other almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners.

The experiments also highlight the relative sensitivity of anytime planners to their tuning parameters. The performance of [RRT]{acronym-label="RRT" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}-style planners depends heavily on the maximum edge length, $\eta$, and achieving the best performance requires tuning it for the problem size, dimension, and even obstacle characteristics. Alternatively, the same batch size was used for [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} on all the tested problems even though further tuning on specific problems could provide better performance (Fig. [16](#fig:fin:batch){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:fin:batch"}). This result should motivate future research on more advanced sample addition procedures, including variable and adaptive batch sizes.

Using heuristics to avoid unnecessary edge evaluations allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to spend more computational effort on the edges that are evaluated. @xie_icra15 show that a [2BVP]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="singular+short"} solver can be used to calculate edges for [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} for problems with differential constraints. They find that doing so is competitive to state-of-the-art optimal sampling-based techniques that are explicitly designed to avoid solving [2BVPs]{acronym-label="2BVP" acronym-form="plural+short"}. @choudhury_icra16 show that a path optimizer [i.e., [CHOMP]{acronym-label="CHOMP" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @zucker_ijrr13] can be used on potential edges in [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}. This provides a method to exploit local problem information (i.e., cost gradients) to propose higher-quality edges and improve performance.

[BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} is described as using a [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"} *ordering* to efficiently search an incrementally built (i.e., changing) [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} embedded in a continuous planning problem. While the search order is the same, it is important to note a key difference in how these two algorithms reuse information. When [LPAstar]{acronym-label="LPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} updates the cost-to-come of a vertex it reconsiders the cost-to-come of all possibly descendent vertices. This can be prohibitively expensive in large graphs and the results of [RRTstar]{acronym-label="RRTstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} demonstrate that this propagation is unnecessary for a planner to almost-surely converge asymptotically to the optimum. By not propagating these changes, [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} performs a truncated rewiring similar to [TLPAstar]{acronym-label="TLPAstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.

This paper demonstrates the benefits of unifying informed graph-based search and sampling-based planning. Using incremental search techniques to efficiently search an increasingly dense [RGG]{acronym-label="RGG" acronym-form="singular+short"} allows [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"} to outperform existing anytime almost-surely asymptotically optimal planners. These results will hopefully motivate further research into combining graph-based search and sampling-based planning. Of particular interest would be probabilistic statements about search efficiency analogous to the formal statements for A\*.

There is also a clear opportunity to consider different anytime approximations, such as deterministic sampling [@janson_ijrr18] or adaptive meshes [@yershov_ijrr16], and more advanced graph-based-search techniques, such as [ARAstar]{acronym-label="ARAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[ARAstar]{acronym-label="ARAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @likhachev_ai08] and [MHAstar]{acronym-label="MHAstar" acronym-form="singular+long"} [[MHAstar]{acronym-label="MHAstar" acronym-form="singular+abbrv"}; @aine_ijrr15], to further accelerate the search performance of [BITstar]{acronym-label="BITstar" acronym-form="singular+short"}.
